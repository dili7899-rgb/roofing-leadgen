# Roof Insurance Claim Process 2026: From Storm Damage to Final Settlement

A roofing contractor and an insurance carrier answer two different questions.

The contractor asks:

**What is physically damaged, and what construction work is required to repair it?**

The insurance company asks:

**What damage resulted from a covered event, what does the policy pay for, and how is that covered loss valued?**

Those questions overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

A well-documented roof claim therefore needs both **technical evidence** and an organized **insurance record**.

## Roof Insurance Claim Process at a Glance

Most residential storm claims follow this sequence:

1. **Identify the storm and date of loss**
2. **Document physical damage**
3. **Prevent additional damage**
4. **Review the policy**
5. **Report the claim**
6. **Complete the adjuster inspection**
7. **Review the carrier estimate line by line**
8. **Compare contractor and insurer scopes**
9. **Submit supplements where justified**
10. **Complete covered repairs**
11. **Request recoverable depreciation if applicable**
12. **Complete mortgage-company requirements**
13. **Reconcile the final payment ledger**

The biggest mistake is treating the first insurance estimate as the end of the process.

It is usually better viewed as the carrier's **initial adjusted scope based on information available at that point**.

# 2026 Roof Insurance Settlement Matrix

The mechanics differ substantially depending on whether the roof is settled at **Replacement Cost Value (RCV)** or **Actual Cash Value (ACV)**.

| Claim phase              | Policy type: RCV vs. ACV                              | Recoverable depreciation rules                                             | Deductible liability                                                     | Carrier / dispute timeline                                              | Out-of-pocket risk                                 |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| **Initial claim report** | Similar process under RCV and ACV                     | No depreciation released yet                                               | Deductible remains homeowner responsibility                              | Report promptly; policy/state deadlines vary                            | Low initially, but delay can create claim problems |
| **Initial adjustment**   | Carrier determines covered scope and replacement cost | Depreciation calculated where applicable                                   | Deductible shown in settlement calculation                               | Claim-handling deadlines vary by state                                  | Depends on deductible and uncovered items          |
| **Initial RCV payment**  | Often starts with an ACV-style payment                | **Recoverable depreciation withheld** until policy requirements are met    | Homeowner still owes deductible                                          | Review policy deadline for completing work and requesting depreciation  | Moderate cash-flow exposure                        |
| **ACV-only settlement**  | Payment reflects depreciated value                    | **Depreciation generally not recoverable**                                 | Homeowner owes deductible plus depreciation gap                          | Often becomes final unless scope/payment is revised                     | **High on older roofs**                            |
| **Supplement review**    | Applies to either valuation method                    | Additional depreciation may be recalculated if scope changes               | Deductible normally is not charged again for the same covered occurrence | No universal national supplement deadline                               | Depends on whether added work is covered           |
| **Completed RCV repair** | Actual completed cost is documented                   | Qualifying withheld depreciation may be released                           | Deductible remains homeowner share                                       | Submit completion documents before policy deadline                      | Lower if scope is fully covered                    |
| **Code upgrade review**  | Depends on Ordinance or Law coverage                  | May change replacement-cost scope                                          | Deductible generally still applies under policy                          | Requires code documentation and carrier review                          | Potentially high without code-upgrade coverage     |
| **Scope dispute**        | Can arise under RCV or ACV                            | Depreciation is secondary to determining correct covered scope             | Deductible generally unchanged                                           | Reinspection, supplement, appraisal, regulatory or legal timelines vary | Can delay construction/final payment               |
| **Final closeout**       | RCV or ACV payments reconciled                        | RCV depreciation released if conditions satisfied; ACV remains depreciated | Homeowner retains contractual deductible                                 | Verify all deadlines before closing file                                | Remaining uncovered work becomes homeowner cost    |

> **Key Point:** RCV coverage usually does **not** mean the entire replacement-cost amount arrives in the first check. Many replacement-cost claims begin with an ACV payment and release qualifying depreciation later.

# Phase 1: Establish the Correct Date of Loss

The **Date of Loss (DOL)** is the date associated with the storm or other event reported as causing the damage.

Possible events include:

* Hail
* Thunderstorm wind
* Tornadic wind
* Hurricane wind
* Falling tree or limb
* Wind-blown debris
* Another covered occurrence

The date matters because the carrier will determine whether coverage was active on that day.

It can also affect claim deadlines.

---

## Create a Date-of-Loss File

Record:

* Calendar date
* Approximate storm time
* Property address
* Whether hail was observed
* Approximate hail size
* Strong-wind observations
* Fallen trees or branches
* Immediate leaks
* Power interruption
* Nearby storm damage

Save original photos and videos whenever possible.

Original files may retain useful timestamp and metadata information.

### Do Not Guess the Date of Loss

If you discover damage weeks later, do not simply choose a storm date because someone tells you that was the "best hailstorm."

Document what you actually know.

Weather reports can support a claimed storm date, but **weather in the area does not prove damage to the individual property**.

---

# Phase 2: Build a Defensible Damage Record

Photographs are strongest when they establish both **location and detail**.

Twenty close-up photos of shingles without identifying the roof slope can be surprisingly difficult to use later.

## Use the Three-Level Photo Method

### 1. Orientation View

Show the entire:

* Roof slope
* Exterior elevation
* Room
* Attic area

### 2. Mid-Range View

Show the damaged item relative to:

* Ridge
* Eave
* Valley
* Vent
* Chimney
* Skylight
* Wall intersection

### 3. Close-Up View

Document the actual:

* Hail impact
* Wind crease
* Missing shingle
* Puncture
* Damaged flashing
* Torn roof covering
* Water stain

Label photographs by location when possible.

Examples:

* North slope
* West elevation
* Garage roof
* Rear valley
* Chimney flashing
* Attic above primary bedroom

---

## Document Collateral Storm Damage

Do not photograph only the shingles.

Check:

* Gutters
* Downspouts
* Metal roof vents
* Drip edge
* Siding
* Window screens
* Garage doors
* HVAC condenser fins
* Exterior trim
* Skylights
* Chimney caps
* Fences
* Fallen trees

Collateral damage can help establish storm exposure and direction.

It does not automatically prove every roof component was damaged.

---

## Document Interior Water Damage Separately

Photograph:

* Ceiling stains
* Wet drywall
* Damaged paint
* Damp insulation
* Wet roof decking
* Water trails
* Flooring damage
* Water near chimneys or vents

A ceiling stain is not necessarily directly below the roof opening.

Water can travel along sheathing, framing, pipes, underlayment, and fasteners.

# Phase 3: Mitigate Additional Damage Without Destroying Evidence

Policies commonly require reasonable action to protect property from additional damage after a loss.

That can include:

* Emergency tarping
* Temporary board-up
* Interior water containment
* Water extraction
* Moving contents
* Temporary stabilization

### Photograph First When Safely Possible

Use this sequence:

**Document → mitigate → preserve receipts → permanently repair**

Do not allow preventable water damage to continue solely because an adjuster has not arrived.

Safety and reasonable mitigation come first.

---

## Keep Emergency Mitigation Receipts

Save invoices for:

* Roof tarping
* Temporary plywood
* Emergency roofing labor
* Water extraction
* Drying equipment
* Electrical inspection
* Temporary lodging where applicable

Temporary work and permanent reconstruction should be separately itemized where practical.

That makes the claim easier to audit.

# Phase 4: Read the Policy Before Assuming the Roof Is Covered

A damaged roof and a covered roof claim are not the same thing.

Review:

* Declarations page
* Dwelling limit
* Base deductible
* Wind/hail deductible
* Hurricane deductible
* Loss Settlement provision
* RCV provisions
* ACV roof endorsements
* Roof payment schedules
* Cosmetic-damage exclusions
* Matching language
* Ordinance or Law coverage
* Duties After Loss
* Appraisal clause
* Legal-action provisions
* Applicable endorsements

The endorsement pages are particularly important.

An endorsement can materially change the coverage described in the main policy form.

# RCV vs. ACV: Identify the Settlement Method Early

## Replacement Cost Value — RCV

RCV generally represents the cost to repair or replace covered damaged property with materials of similar kind and quality at current cost, subject to policy terms and limits.

Many RCV claims involve **two payment stages**.

The carrier may initially pay the depreciated value and hold the remaining depreciation until qualifying repairs are completed.

---

## Actual Cash Value — ACV

ACV generally accounts for:

* Age
* Condition
* Wear
* Depreciation

The result can be substantially lower than the amount required to replace the roof.

For an ACV-only roof endorsement, depreciation may remain permanently the homeowner's responsibility.

# 2026 Filing Deadline Trigger: Assume the Clock Matters

> **Strict Filing Deadline Trigger:** There is **no universal nationwide deadline** for roof insurance claims.

Separate deadlines can apply to:

* Initial notice of loss
* Proof of loss
* Reopened claims
* Supplements
* Recoverable depreciation
* Appraisal demands
* Lawsuits

Those deadlines can come from both **policy language and state law**.

### Treat the Date of Loss as Day Zero

As soon as a potentially covered loss is identified:

1. Record the date.
2. Review the policy.
3. Report promptly.
4. Save the claim acknowledgment.
5. Record every deadline communicated by the carrier.

Do not assume you can wait indefinitely because the roof is not actively leaking.

# Phase 6: Prepare for the Adjuster Walkthrough

Try to attend the inspection when practical.

The goal is not to argue for a predetermined settlement.

It is to make sure the adjuster has access to the relevant evidence.

Have available:

* Storm date
* Exterior photos
* Interior photos
* Emergency repair invoices
* Contractor inspection report
* Roof measurements
* Known leak locations

Point out all affected areas.

That may include more than the roof.

---

## Areas the Adjuster May Evaluate

### Coverage Issues

The carrier may consider:

* Did a covered event occur?
* Was the policy active?
* Is the damage consistent with that event?
* Is deterioration also present?
* Does an exclusion apply?
* Does a roof endorsement limit settlement?
* Does a special deductible apply?

### Scope Issues

If coverage applies, the adjuster may consider:

* Which roof slopes are affected?
* How many shingles are damaged?
* Is localized repair possible?
* Are ridge or hip materials involved?
* Was flashing damaged?
* Is underlayment replacement required?
* Is decking damaged?
* Are gutters or siding involved?
* Are code-related operations triggered?

Coverage and scope are separate questions.

---

# Should the Roofing Contractor Attend the Adjuster Inspection?

A contractor can often provide useful **construction information**.

That may include:

* Roof measurements
* Damage photographs
* Observed physical conditions
* Material identification
* Repair requirements

The contractor should remain within the legal boundaries applicable in the state.

In some jurisdictions, negotiating coverage or claim settlement on behalf of a homeowner can constitute regulated public-adjusting activity.

A contractor is not automatically the homeowner's insurance representative.

# Phase 7: Read the Insurance Estimate Line by Line

Do not evaluate a roof settlement from the bottom-line number alone.

A $17,500 estimate is not necessarily "better" than a $16,000 estimate if the underlying scopes are different.

Start with the construction operations.

---

# Understanding Xactimate Roof Estimates

Xactimate is widely used for property-loss estimating.

It breaks construction work into individual line items involving quantities, units, operations, and regional pricing data.

A roof estimate can include separate operations for:

* Tear-off
* Shingles
* Underlayment
* Ice-and-water protection
* Starter course
* Ridge and hip caps
* Pipe flashing
* Roof vents
* Drip edge
* Detach-and-reset work
* Steep-roof operations
* Disposal

### Xactimate Is an Estimating Tool, Not the Insurance Policy

A line item appearing in Xactimate does not automatically make it covered.

Likewise, a necessary construction operation does not become unnecessary simply because it was omitted from the first estimate.

The practical question is:

**Is the operation required for the covered repair, and does the policy pay for it?**

---

# Scope First, Unit Price Second

Many "pricing disputes" are actually **scope disputes**.

Before arguing about a price per square, compare quantities.

## Check:

* Total roof area
* Number of squares
* Number of existing layers
* Roof slopes included
* Ridge length
* Hip length
* Valley length
* Eave footage
* Rake footage
* Drip-edge footage
* Number of vents
* Number of pipe penetrations
* Skylights
* Waste percentage
* Steepness
* Height/access conditions

A missing 300-square-foot roof plane has a larger effect than a small difference in shingle unit cost.

---

# Common Roof Scope Items to Reconcile

Compare the contractor and carrier estimates for:

| Roof component           | What to verify                                |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------- |
| **Tear-off**             | Number of layers and total squares            |
| **Replacement shingles** | Material type and quantity                    |
| **Waste**                | Roof geometry and material requirements       |
| **Starter**              | Eave/rake quantities where required           |
| **Ridge / hip caps**     | Linear footage and product                    |
| **Underlayment**         | Type and roof area                            |
| **Ice barrier**          | Required locations and quantity               |
| **Drip edge**            | Eave/rake footage and code applicability      |
| **Valleys**              | Valley construction method                    |
| **Pipe boots**           | Number and type                               |
| **Flashing**             | Reuse vs. replacement requirement             |
| **Ventilation**          | Existing vs. required system                  |
| **Decking**              | Covered damage vs. pre-existing deterioration |
| **Permits**              | Local permit requirement                      |
| **Detach/reset**         | Solar, gutters, satellite equipment, etc.     |
| **Steep/high charges**   | Roof pitch and access                         |
| **Interior damage**      | Ceiling, insulation, paint, flooring          |

This comparison is far more useful than arguing only over the estimate total.

# Phase 8: Understand the RCV Depreciation Holdback

This is the part of the claim process that confuses many homeowners.

Assume the insurer determines:

* Covered replacement cost: **$20,000**
* Depreciation: **$6,000**
* Deductible: **$2,500**

## Step 1: Calculate ACV

`$20,000 RCV - $6,000 depreciation = $14,000 ACV`

## Step 2: Apply the Deductible

`$14,000 ACV - $2,500 deductible = $11,500 initial payment`

The homeowner initially receives approximately:

**$11,500**

The carrier has not necessarily denied the remaining $6,000.

It may be **recoverable depreciation being held back**.

---

## Step 3: Complete the Qualifying Repairs

The homeowner completes the covered work according to the policy requirements.

Documentation may include:

* Contractor invoice
* Signed contract
* Paid receipts
* Completion photographs
* Permit closeout
* Certificate of completion
* Mortgage inspection

Requirements vary by insurer and policy.

---

## Step 4: Request Recoverable Depreciation

After qualifying work is complete, submit the required documents.

If the policy conditions are satisfied, the insurer may release some or all of the withheld depreciation.

In the simplified example:

**Potential depreciation release: $6,000**

Final insurance proceeds could therefore total:

`$11,500 + $6,000 = $17,500`

The remaining:

**$2,500**

is the homeowner's deductible.

---

# Why the Final RCV Check May Be Less Than the Holdback

Recoverable depreciation is not necessarily an automatic lump sum regardless of actual construction cost.

Policies often limit payment to amounts actually and necessarily spent, subject to the policy.

For example, if:

* Carrier RCV estimate = $20,000
* Contractor actually completes equivalent covered work for substantially less

the final recoverable amount can depend on the policy's loss-settlement language and actual incurred cost.

Do not assume the homeowner receives the entire theoretical replacement-cost estimate as profit.

---

# ACV-Only Roof Example

Now assume the same numbers under an ACV-only roof endorsement:

* Replacement cost: $20,000
* Depreciation: $6,000
* Deductible: $2,500

The calculation remains:

`$20,000 - $6,000 - $2,500 = $11,500`

But in an ACV-only settlement, the $6,000 depreciation may **not** be recoverable later.

The homeowner could therefore face:

**$8,500 of total replacement funding responsibility**

consisting of:

* $6,000 depreciation
* $2,500 deductible

That is why identifying roof-specific ACV endorsements early is critical.

---

# The Deductible Remains the Homeowner's Share

The deductible is the policyholder's contractual portion of the covered loss.

It can be structured as:

* Flat dollar amount
* Percentage deductible
* Wind deductible
* Hail deductible
* Hurricane deductible
* Roof-specific deductible where permitted

### A 2% Deductible May Be Much Larger Than Expected

A percentage deductible often applies to an insured value such as the dwelling limit—not 2% of the contractor invoice.

Example:

Dwelling limit:

**$400,000**

2% wind/hail deductible:

`$400,000 × 0.02 = $8,000`

Always read the declarations page.

---

# Do Not Build a Contract Around "Waiving the Deductible"

Contractor and insurance laws vary by state.

Several states expressly prohibit contractors from absorbing, rebating, or disguising a homeowner's deductible.

Red flags include statements such as:

* "We'll eat your deductible."
* "We'll credit it back."
* "We'll raise the invoice so insurance covers it."
* "You won't pay anything."

A legitimate repair contract should clearly identify the actual work and actual price.

---

# Phase 9: Compare the Contractor Estimate With the Carrier Estimate

Different totals do not automatically mean someone is acting improperly.

Differences can result from:

* Different measurements
* Different waste calculations
* Different material specifications
* Missing accessories
* Flashing requirements
* Permit fees
* Decking
* Ventilation
* Steepness
* Access
* Local supplier pricing
* Additional storm damage
* Hidden conditions

Use a line-by-line reconciliation.

### Ask Five Questions

1. **Is the same physical work included?**
2. **Are the quantities the same?**
3. **Are the materials equivalent?**
4. **Are required construction operations included?**
5. **Is the disagreement scope, pricing, or coverage?**

Those are three very different disputes.

# Phase 10: Use the Supplement Process Correctly

A supplement is additional information submitted for review after the initial adjustment.

A legitimate supplement explains why additional covered work or cost may be necessary.

It is not simply:

**"The contractor wants more money."**

---

## Strong Supplement Documentation Can Include

* Revised itemized estimate
* Roof measurements
* Photographs
* Tear-off photos
* Manufacturer installation requirements
* Material invoices
* Building-code sections
* Permit information
* Building-department correspondence
* Decking measurements
* Concealed-condition documentation

The carrier then determines whether the additional requested amount is covered.

---

## Common Roof Supplement Categories

Possible supplemental items include:

* Additional roof layer discovered
* Hidden storm damage
* Covered decking damage
* Revised roof measurements
* Flashing reconstruction
* Ice barrier
* Drip edge
* Ventilation
* Permit fees
* Additional siding damage
* Additional gutter damage
* Material price differences supported by documentation

Some of these may be covered.

Others may be maintenance, pre-existing deterioration, optional upgrades, or otherwise excluded.

---

# Hidden Decking Damage During Tear-Off

Roof demolition often reveals conditions that could not be fully inspected beforehand.

Examples include:

* Rotten OSB
* Delaminated plywood
* Broken boards
* Old repairs
* Multiple roof layers
* Wet insulation
* Improper flashing
* Storm punctures

The critical insurance issue becomes **causation**.

Old rot and storm-created deck damage are not necessarily treated the same way.

---

## Hidden-Damage Documentation Protocol

When practical:

1. Stop in the affected area.
2. Photograph before removal.
3. Measure the damaged section.
4. Record material thickness/type.
5. Explain why replacement is required.
6. Notify the insurer if a supplement may be appropriate.
7. Save invoices.
8. Preserve permit/code documentation.

Do not replace several hundred square feet of disputed decking and then rely only on a verbal description afterward.

---

# 2026 IRC R908 / "25% Rule" Trigger

> **Code Trigger:** IRC R908 governs reroofing, but it does **not** create a universal nationwide rule requiring full roof replacement whenever more than 25% of a roof is damaged.

Percentage-based triggers can exist through:

* State codes
* Local amendments
* Existing-building codes
* Special windstorm provisions
* Jurisdiction-specific rules

If a contractor, adjuster, or building department invokes a 25% threshold, request the exact adopted provision.

### Ask for:

* Code edition
* Section number
* Local amendment
* Definition of the roof/roof section
* Explanation of how the current repair triggers it

Do not submit a model-code citation that the municipality has not adopted.

---

# Code Compliance and Insurance Are Separate Questions

A building official can require certain construction work.

That does not automatically mean the insurance contract pays for all of it.

This distinction is fundamental.

## Construction Question

**What does the adopted code require for the reroof?**

## Insurance Question

**Does the policy cover the increased cost of complying with that requirement?**

That is where **Ordinance or Law coverage** becomes important.

---

# Ordinance or Law Coverage: The Code-Upgrade Trigger

> **2026 Ordinance or Law Trigger:** Code-required upgrades can create costs beyond simply replacing damaged shingles. Whether those costs are insured depends on the policy's Ordinance or Law coverage, limits, endorsements, and applicable law.

Potential code-related items can include:

* Drip edge
* Ice-and-water protection
* Roof deck attachment
* Underlayment
* Flashing
* Ventilation
* Deck corrections
* Other locally required components

Do not assume every code item is automatically covered.

---

## How to Support a Code Supplement

A strong submission identifies:

1. **Exact code requirement**
2. **Adopted code edition**
3. **Local amendment where applicable**
4. **Why the current work triggers compliance**
5. **Quantity**
6. **Labor/material cost**
7. **Applicable Ordinance or Law coverage**

The phrase:

**"It's required by code"**

is much weaker than:

**"The local building department requires this operation under the adopted provision, and here is the quantity and cost."**

---

# Example: Drip Edge Code Supplement

Suppose the old roof does not have drip edge.

The jurisdiction requires drip edge when the roof is replaced.

The contractor may document:

* Applicable section
* Eave footage
* Rake footage
* Material
* Installation operation
* Permit requirement

The insurer then determines whether the policy pays the additional code-compliance cost.

Again:

**Code requirement ≠ automatic insurance coverage.**

# Phase 11: Know the Carrier Dispute Path

Not every disagreement requires a lawsuit.

The correct escalation depends on **what is actually disputed**.

## Scope / Amount Dispute

Examples:

* Roof is covered, but quantities differ
* Carrier omitted flashing
* Parties disagree on roof area
* Contractor documents additional covered operations

Possible next steps can include:

* Supplement
* Reinspection
* Adjuster review
* Supervisor review
* Appraisal where available and appropriate

---

## Coverage Dispute

Examples:

* Carrier says damage is wear and tear
* Homeowner says it is hail
* Carrier invokes an exclusion
* Policy interpretation is disputed

That is different from arguing about $500 of ridge-cap quantity.

Possible options may include:

* Reconsideration
* Additional evidence
* State insurance complaint
* Licensed public adjuster where appropriate
* Attorney consultation

Applicable procedures vary by state and policy.

---

# Appraisal: Amount of Loss vs. Coverage

Many policies contain an appraisal provision.

Appraisal commonly addresses disagreement over the **amount of loss**.

For example:

**Carrier agrees hail damage is covered but estimates $12,000; homeowner contends covered repair costs $20,000.**

That is different from:

**Carrier says the roof damage is excluded entirely.**

Appraisal procedure and scope vary by state and contract.

Read the exact provision before assuming appraisal resolves every dispute.

---

# Claim Deadlines and Lawsuit Deadlines Are Not the Same Thing

A homeowner can face several separate clocks.

| Deadline                   | What it governs                                     |
| -------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| **Notice of loss**         | When the insurer must be told about the event       |
| **Proof of loss**          | Formal documentation required by some policies      |
| **Supplement deadline**    | When additional damage/cost must be submitted       |
| **Replacement deadline**   | When work must be completed to recover depreciation |
| **Appraisal deadline**     | Timing for invoking contractual dispute process     |
| **Suit limitation**        | Contractual time limit for legal action             |
| **Statute of limitations** | State-law deadline affecting legal rights           |

Do not treat these as interchangeable.

Record every deadline in the claim file.

# Phase 12: Mortgage Company and Insurance Checks

Homeowners are often surprised when an insurance check also names the mortgage servicer.

The lender has a financial interest in the property.

For larger losses, the servicer may hold or control some repair funds.

---

## Typical Loss-Draft Requirements

A mortgage servicer may request:

* Insurance check
* Contractor agreement
* Repair estimate
* Contractor information
* Tax form
* Insurance certificate
* Lien waiver
* Progress inspection
* Final inspection

Requirements vary by servicer.

Call the mortgage company **before the contractor needs the money**.

---

## Funds May Be Released in Stages

A common process is:

1. Initial repair funds
2. Progress release
3. Final release after inspection

This can create cash-flow issues if the contractor expects a larger payment before the mortgage servicer releases it.

Coordinate the payment schedule early.

# Contractor Contracts During an Insurance Claim

Do not sign a contract simply because it says:

**"Insurance approved."**

The roofing agreement should still identify:

* Contractor legal name
* Scope
* Roofing product
* Underlayment
* Flashing
* Permit responsibility
* Price
* Deductible responsibility
* Payment schedule
* Supplement/change-order process
* Workmanship warranty

---

## Be Careful With "All Insurance Proceeds" Contracts

A contract that simply states:

**"Homeowner agrees to pay contractor all insurance proceeds"**

can be difficult to evaluate because the final claim may include items beyond the roofer's actual scope.

Prefer a defined construction agreement.

Know what work the contractor is being paid to perform.

---

# Contractor vs. Public Adjuster: Keep the Roles Clear

A roofing contractor can generally provide construction facts such as:

* Measurements
* Photographs
* Material specifications
* Repair requirements
* Pricing

Representing the homeowner in coverage negotiations can be regulated differently.

Public adjusting is a licensed activity in many states.

Some jurisdictions specifically restrict a contractor from acting as both contractor and public adjuster on the same claim.

Verify local rules before authorizing someone to "handle your insurance claim" on your behalf.

---

# What Not to Do During a Roof Claim

Avoid actions that create unnecessary claim problems.

## Do Not:

* Manufacture hail damage
* Bend shingles to create wind creases
* Scrape away granules
* Exaggerate the storm date
* Submit inflated invoices
* Hide contractor discounts
* Agree to a fake deductible waiver
* Throw away important evidence before documenting it
* Assume the contractor determines coverage
* Assume the first carrier estimate is automatically final

The goal is to document and recover the **legitimate covered loss**.

Nothing more—and nothing less.

---

# If the Roof Claim Is Partially Paid

A partial payment can occur because the carrier:

* Approved only certain roof slopes
* Approved repairs rather than replacement
* Withheld depreciation
* Applied the deductible
* Excluded certain operations
* Has not reviewed a supplement
* Applied a roof endorsement

Start by reconciling the estimate.

Do not assume the missing money has one single cause.

---

# If the Roof Claim Is Denied

Obtain the decision in writing.

Identify:

* Reason stated
* Policy provisions cited
* Damage the carrier accepted or rejected
* Whether the issue is causation, exclusion, notice, or documentation

Then build any response around that exact issue.

### Example

If the denial states:

**"Observed conditions are consistent with long-term deterioration rather than hail."**

Relevant additional evidence might include:

* Dated storm documentation
* Collateral hail impacts
* Close-up roof photographs
* Contractor findings
* Previous roof-condition documentation

Simply sending the same estimate again may not address the stated reason.

# Frequently Asked Questions

## Does RCV Mean Insurance Pays for My Entire Roof Up Front?

Usually not.

Many RCV claims begin with payment based on the roof's actual cash value.

The carrier withholds qualifying depreciation until repair or replacement requirements are satisfied.

The deductible remains the homeowner's responsibility.

---

## What Is Recoverable Depreciation?

Recoverable depreciation is depreciation initially withheld under a qualifying replacement-cost policy that may become payable after required repairs are completed.

The carrier may request:

* Final contractor invoice
* Completion photographs
* Paid receipts
* Permit documentation

Check the policy's deadline for completing work and requesting the funds.

---

## Why Is My First Insurance Check So Much Lower Than the Roof Estimate?

Common reasons include:

* Depreciation withheld
* Deductible applied
* ACV roof endorsement
* Limited carrier scope
* Some work not yet approved
* Mortgage-company involvement
* Supplement not yet reviewed

Reconcile the settlement statement before concluding that the difference is entirely depreciation.

---

## Does ACV Mean I Cannot Recover Depreciation Later?

If the roof is settled strictly on an ACV basis under the applicable policy or endorsement, depreciation is generally not recoverable simply because you later replace the roof.

That is different from an RCV claim where depreciation is temporarily withheld.

Read the actual Loss Settlement provision and roof endorsements.

---

## Can My Roofing Contractor Negotiate With the Insurance Company?

Contractors can generally provide factual construction information such as measurements, photographs, material requirements, and estimates.

The ability to negotiate insurance coverage or settlement on behalf of a homeowner can be regulated as public adjusting.

Rules vary by state.

Do not assume every roofer advertising that it "handles insurance" is legally authorized to represent you in all claim matters.

---

## What Is Xactimate?

Xactimate is construction-estimating software widely used in property claims.

It organizes repair work into measurable line items and can apply geographic/date-sensitive pricing information.

The important homeowner question is not:

**"Did they use Xactimate?"**

It is:

**"Does the estimate include the correct covered construction scope and quantities?"**

---

## Can I Challenge an Xactimate Price?

A contractor can document why its actual construction cost differs from the carrier estimate.

But first determine whether the disagreement is truly pricing.

Common differences involve:

* Missing operations
* Measurements
* Waste
* Flashing
* Permits
* Access
* Material specification

The carrier then evaluates any supplemental documentation under the policy.

---

## What Is a Roof Insurance Supplement?

A supplement is additional documentation requesting review of covered scope or cost not fully addressed in the initial adjustment.

A good supplement connects the requested amount to a specific construction need.

Examples include:

* Hidden covered damage
* Corrected measurements
* Required flashing
* Code-related construction
* Permit costs

A supplement is not automatically payable.

---

## Does the IRC 25% Rule Force Insurance to Buy My Entire Roof?

No.

There is no universal nationwide IRC R908 rule requiring complete roof replacement whenever 25% of a roof is damaged.

A state or local jurisdiction may have its own threshold or amendment.

Even when code requires additional construction, insurance payment depends on the policy, including applicable Ordinance or Law coverage.

---

## What Is Ordinance or Law Coverage?

Ordinance or Law coverage can pay qualifying increased construction costs caused by enforcement of current building codes after a covered loss.

Examples can include required upgrades to:

* Underlayment
* Drip edge
* Deck attachment
* Flashing
* Other code-driven components

Coverage limits and conditions vary.

The code requirement and the insurance payment obligation are separate questions.

---

## Can the Contractor Waive My Deductible?

Do not assume so.

Several states expressly prohibit contractors from waiving, absorbing, rebating, or disguising insurance deductibles.

Even where rules differ, intentionally inflating an invoice to make an insurer unknowingly fund the deductible can create serious legal and insurance problems.

Pay the contractual deductible and retain proof.

---

## Why Is My Mortgage Company Listed on the Insurance Check?

The mortgage lender has a financial interest in the property.

For significant claims, checks are commonly made payable to both the homeowner and mortgage servicer.

The servicer may release funds as repairs progress.

Contact the mortgage company early to learn its loss-draft procedure.

---

## What Should I Do if the Contractor Finds Rotten Decking?

Document it before replacement.

The roofer should:

1. Photograph the decking.
2. Measure the affected area.
3. Explain why replacement is necessary.
4. Identify whether it is old deterioration or potentially storm-related.
5. Document any applicable code requirement.
6. Submit relevant information for carrier review where appropriate.

Long-term rot and storm-created physical damage may be handled differently under the policy.

---

## How Long Do I Have to File a Roof Claim?

There is no nationwide answer.

Deadlines can differ by:

* State
* Policy
* Cause of loss
* Initial vs. supplemental claim
* Proof-of-loss requirement
* Legal action

Report suspected covered damage promptly and review the actual policy.

Do not rely on a generic online statement that you "always have one year" or "always have two years."

---

# Bottom Line: Manage the Roof Claim as a Documented Process

A strong roof insurance claim is not built around the phrase:

**"Insurance owes me a new roof."**

It is built around five questions:

1. **What covered event occurred?**
2. **What physical damage resulted from it?**
3. **What construction work is reasonably required?**
4. **What does the policy pay for?**
5. **What documentation is required to receive the remaining funds?**

For most homeowners, the safest sequence is:

**Document the loss → mitigate further damage → report promptly → attend the inspection → reconcile the scope → document supplements → complete covered work → recover eligible depreciation → close the claim file.**

That approach helps homeowners capture legitimate covered construction costs without confusing roofing scope, insurance coverage, code requirements, or contractor contracts.
