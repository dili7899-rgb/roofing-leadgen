# Roof Inspection Checklist Before Buying a House: 2026 Buyer Guide

A roof that looks acceptable from the driveway can still create a **$10,000+ post-closing liability**.

The expensive problems are often not obvious missing shingles. They are brittle roofing, failed flashing, concealed deck rot, poor attic ventilation, multiple reroof layers, structural alterations, and a roof your new insurer will not accept on favorable terms.

Before the inspection contingency expires, the roof evaluation should answer four questions:

1. **Is it leaking or structurally compromised now?**
2. **How much useful service life is realistically left?**
3. **What will known and concealed defects cost to correct?**
4. **Will the lender and homeowners insurer accept the roof in its current condition?**

The goal is not simply to decide whether the roof is "good" or "bad."

The goal is to convert roof condition into a **transaction number and closing decision**.

# Three 2026 Roof Triggers Every Homebuyer Should Understand

## Insurance Trigger: The 10-Year Roof Is an Underwriting Checkpoint

> **2026 Insurance Trigger — 10+ Year Roofs:** There is **no nationwide rule automatically converting every roof to ACV at 10 years**.

Some insurers use roof age as an underwriting threshold and may shift an aging roof from **Replacement Cost Value (RCV)** coverage to **Actual Cash Value (ACV)** or a depreciation schedule.

Others may:

* Continue RCV coverage
* Require a roof inspection
* Increase premiums
* Apply a roof payment schedule
* Increase wind/hail deductibles
* Exclude cosmetic damage
* Require roof replacement
* Refuse the risk altogether

Treat **10 years as an insurance-shopping checkpoint**, not an automatic coverage cutoff.

### Why ACV Matters

Assume a future covered roof loss costs **$15,000** to repair.

With RCV coverage, depreciation may ultimately be recoverable according to policy terms.

With ACV coverage, the insurer subtracts depreciation based on age and condition.

A technically serviceable older roof can therefore still create a major **future insurance funding gap**.

---

## Code Trigger: IRC R908 Does Not Create a Nationwide "25% Rule"

> **2026 Code Trigger:** The IRC does **not** contain a universal national rule stating that damage or repair to more than 25% of a residential roof automatically requires full replacement.

IRC Section R908 regulates **reroofing**, including roof replacement, roof recovering, structural load capability, deteriorated substrates, existing layers, and flashing reconstruction.

States and municipalities can adopt:

* Local amendments
* Existing-building rules
* Percentage-based thresholds
* Hurricane-region provisions
* Different code editions

A 25% threshold may apply in a particular jurisdiction.

It should never be assumed nationally.

### If Someone Cites the "25% Rule"

Ask for:

1. The exact code section
2. The adopted code edition
3. State or municipal amendments
4. The definition of the affected roof or roof section
5. Any applicable exception

That is especially important when a seller, contractor, or insurer is using code to justify or reject a larger scope of work.

---

## Structural Trigger: Roof Dead Load Matters

IRC reroofing provisions require the roof structure to support the roof-covering system and loads encountered during installation.

This matters when the house has:

* Multiple roof layers
* Concrete or clay tile
* Slate
* Heavy overlays
* Solar equipment
* Historical structural modifications
* Significant snow loading

> **Structural Dead-Load Trigger:** If a lightweight asphalt roof was converted to a substantially heavier roof system, ask for the **product weight in PSF and structural documentation**.

Do not assume rafters or trusses are adequate simply because the heavy roof has remained in place for several years.

* Insurance-related roof documentation supplied by the seller

Do not rely on phrases such as:

**"Newer roof."**

## Find Out What "New Roof" Actually Means

Ask whether the work was:

* A full tear-off to the roof deck
* An overlay over existing shingles
* Replacement of one slope only
* Storm-damage repair
* New shingles installed over reused flashing
* A roof coating
* Replacement on an addition only

These projects can all appear in a listing as "roof updated."

They create very different liabilities.

# Verify Roof Age Using More Than the Seller's Memory

Use the strongest documentation available.

A practical hierarchy is:

1. **Permit date**
2. **Paid contractor invoice**
3. Manufacturer warranty registration
4. Seller disclosure
5. Insurance documentation
6. Prior MLS listings
7. Historical aerial/street imagery
8. Physical age estimate

Physical appearance is useful.

It is not proof of installation date.

### Why Age Verification Matters

Roof age can affect:

* Remaining useful life
* Repairability
* Insurance eligibility
* ACV vs. RCV settlement
* Lender concerns
* Resale expectations

If nobody can document the roof age, treat it as **unverified**, not as whatever date best fits the transaction.

# Exterior Roof Surface & Geometry Checklist

Start with the overall structure before inspecting individual shingles.

Stand far enough away to evaluate:

* Ridge
* Eaves
* Rakes
* Hips
* Valleys
* Dormers
* Additions
* Roof-plane transitions

The shape of the roof can reveal problems the roofing material hides.

---

## Ridge-Line Sag

A noticeable dip or curve in the ridge deserves investigation.

Possible causes include:

* Long-term rafter deflection
* Undersized framing
* Rafter spread
* Damaged trusses
* Altered trusses
* Deteriorated wood
* Bearing movement
* Excessive roof weight
* Historical snow loading

Do not diagnose the cause from the driveway.

Correlate the exterior shape with the attic framing.

---

## Wavy or Uneven Roof Planes

Waviness between framing members can suggest:

* Swollen OSB
* Deteriorated plywood
* Thin sheathing
* Improper panel spacing
* Uneven framing
* Multiple roofing layers
* Prior deck patches

Architectural shingles naturally create texture.

Look for actual plane deformation beneath the roofing.

---

# Remaining Useful Life: Do Not Calculate It From Age Alone

Remaining Useful Life, or **RUL**, is not:

`Manufacturer warranty - calendar age`

A 17-year-old architectural roof can have meaningful useful life remaining if it has:

* Intact shingles
* Limited granule depletion
* Dry decking
* Functional flashing
* Proper attic conditions
* Limited storm history

Another 17-year-old roof may be near replacement because it has:

* Widespread cracking
* Curling
* Brittle tabs
* Failed seals
* Multiple repairs
* Two layers
* Repeated leaks
* Soft decking

Condition should control the assessment.

---

# Asphalt Shingle Surface Inspection

## Granule Loss

Granules protect the asphalt surface from direct UV exposure.

Look for:

* Widespread exposed asphalt
* Bare shingle edges
* Uneven depletion
* Wear in drainage paths
* Excess gutter granules
* Reinforcement becoming visible

Granules in gutters alone are not a roof-failure diagnosis.

Normal aging, installation, walking, storms, trees, and mechanical abrasion can all release granules.

### Transaction Red Flag

The concern becomes more significant when granule depletion appears together with:

* Cracking
* Brittleness
* Curling
* Exposed asphalt
* Failed seals

That combination suggests declining repairability and reduced remaining life.

---

## Curling and Cupping

Look for shingle corners or tabs turning upward or downward.

Possible contributors include:

* Aging
* Moisture
* Installation conditions
* Deck movement
* Ventilation problems
* Material characteristics

A few distorted shingles may be repairable.

Systemic distortion across multiple roof slopes is a replacement-level warning sign.

---

## Cracked or Split Shingles

Distinguish between:

* Surface checking
* Thermal cracking
* Wind creases
* Hail fractures
* Foot-traffic damage
* Complete splits

One cracked shingle beside a vent is not equivalent to cracking across several roof slopes.

Distribution matters.

---

## Shingle Seal Condition

Asphalt shingles depend partly on adhesive seals to resist wind uplift.

Look for:

* Raised tabs
* Uneven lower edges
* Wind creases
* Repeated hand sealing
* Large areas that appear unbonded

A roof can remain visually complete while having poor wind resistance because its seals have deteriorated.

---

# Ridge and Hip Condition

Ridge and hip shingles often receive greater:

* UV exposure
* Wind exposure
* Foot traffic
* Mechanical stress

Inspect for:

* Cracked caps
* Missing caps
* Exposed fasteners
* Loose ridge material
* Heavy granule loss
* Improper repairs

Do not ignore ridge-cap deterioration just because the field shingles look acceptable.

---

# Flashing Inspection: Where Expensive Leaks Often Begin

Many roof leaks occur at transitions—not in the middle of a roof plane.

## Chimney Flashing

Inspect:

* Apron flashing
* Step flashing
* Counterflashing
* Back-pan/cricket configuration
* Masonry joints

Red flags include:

* Heavy roofing cement
* Tar patches
* Rust
* Exposed nails
* Loose counterflashing
* Missing step flashing
* Attic staining beside the chimney

A thick bead of sealant usually proves somebody worked on the area.

It does not prove the flashing was permanently repaired.

---

## Step Flashing

Where the roof meets a sidewall, flashing should work with successive roofing courses to drain water outward.

Warning signs include:

* Missing pieces
* Rust
* Poor overlap
* Exposed nails
* Siding pressed tightly against roofing
* Large sealant patches

Repeated sealant repairs can indicate a drainage detail that was never correctly reconstructed.

---

## Kick-Out Flashing

Kick-out flashing directs concentrated roof runoff away from the wall and into the gutter.

When absent or defective, water can move behind:

* Siding
* Stucco
* Sheathing
* Wall framing

Inspect the lower roof-to-wall intersection for swelling, staining, or rot.

---

## Plumbing Vent Boots

Look for:

* Cracked rubber
* Split collars
* UV deterioration
* Rust
* Exposed fasteners
* Thick sealant repairs

A deteriorated pipe boot is often a relatively inexpensive repair.

The concealed water damage beneath it may not be.

---

## Valleys

Valleys carry concentrated runoff.

Inspect for:

* Debris
* Worn roofing
* Open cuts
* Exposed fasteners
* Patch material
* Corroded metal
* Misaligned shingles

Treat valley defects more seriously than equivalent wear in a low-flow area of the roof.

---

# Check How Many Roofing Layers Exist

A second roof layer changes both inspection confidence and future cost.

An overlay can conceal:

* Original shingles
* Existing flashing
* Roof-deck defects
* Previous patching
* Old underlayment
* Historical leakage

It also adds permanent dead load.

### 2024 IRC Reroofing Issue

Under the current IRC framework, roof recovering is not permitted where the existing roof:

* Is water-soaked or too deteriorated to provide an adequate base
* Uses certain existing heavy roof coverings
* Already has **two or more roof-covering applications**

Local adoption controls.

For a buyer, the important financial implication is simple:

**The next roof may require complete tear-off.**

---

## Multi-Layer Roof Cost Exposure

Tearing off existing shingles typically adds roughly:

**$100–$300 per roofing square**

A 25-square roof could therefore carry approximately:

**$2,500–$7,500 in tear-off exposure**

before the new roof system is installed.

Multiple layers also increase the chance that concealed deck repairs will be discovered after removal.

---

# Inspect Gutters, Fascia, and Soffits

## Gutters

Look for:

* Standing water
* Improper slope
* Loose hangers
* Leaking joints
* Overflow marks
* Heavy granule accumulation
* Corrosion

Overflow can damage fascia and roof-edge decking even when the main roof covering is intact.

---

## Fascia

Red flags include:

* Peeling paint
* Swelling
* Soft wood
* Dark stains
* Separation
* Repeated patches

Fascia rot can indicate gutter leakage or defective roof-edge drainage.

---

## Soffits

Inspect for:

* Rot
* Water stains
* Missing panels
* Animal openings
* Painted-over vents
* Blocked intake openings

A soffit can appear to contain ventilation while insulation blocks all actual airflow inside the attic.

---

# Attic & Structural Ventilation Audit

The attic is often where the most expensive defects become visible.

A dry day outside does not erase evidence of years of leakage or condensation.

Do not step directly on drywall between joists or truss chords.

---

## Inspect Roof Sheathing

### Plywood Red Flags

Look for:

* Dark staining
* Veneer separation
* Delamination
* Soft spots
* Sagging
* Fastener deterioration

### OSB Red Flags

Look for:

* Swollen edges
* Flaking strands
* Soft surfaces
* Sagging
* Persistent swelling
* Poor fastener holding

Temporary moisture does not automatically mean structural replacement is necessary.

Soft or delaminated wood is a much more serious condition.

---

# Trace Historical Water Intrusion

Old leaks may not be wet on inspection day.

Look for:

* Stained sheathing
* Dark rafters
* Rusted nail tips
* Mineral deposits
* Wet or discolored insulation
* Water trails
* Ceiling patches
* Stains beside chimneys or vents

Follow evidence **upslope**.

Water can travel along decking, rafters, pipes, and fasteners before appearing inside the home.

---

# Check Rafters and Trusses for Structural Alterations

## Engineered Trusses

Look for:

* Cut webs
* Drilled chords
* Removed members
* Cracked lumber
* Broken connector plates
* Improvised braces
* HVAC ducts routed through cut members
* Heavy attic storage

Engineered trusses should not be casually cut or modified.

If modifications exist without documentation, request an engineer's evaluation.

---

## Conventional Rafters

Inspect for:

* Splits
* Excessive bowing
* Improper notches
* Large drilled holes
* Poor bearing
* Displaced ridge connections
* Improvised sister repairs

A sistered rafter is not automatically defective.

The question is whether the repair actually transfers the required structural loads.

---

# Dead Load: Do Not Ignore Heavy Roof Conversions

A roof structure carries permanent weight before snow, workers, solar panels, or other temporary loads are added.

A buyer should investigate when the home was converted from a lightweight roof to:

* Concrete tile
* Clay tile
* Slate
* Multiple roofing layers

Request:

* Exact roof product
* Published product weight
* Structural design documentation
* Permit history
* Engineering report where applicable

Never assume "it's been there for 15 years" proves adequate structural design.

---

# Moisture Meter Readings: Use Them as Evidence, Not a Verdict

Moisture meters are useful for comparing suspect wood with nearby dry areas.

A single reading should not automatically determine whether decking needs replacement.

Wood condition should be evaluated with:

* Moisture level
* Visual staining
* Softness
* Delamination
* Fungal growth
* Weather before inspection
* Nearby reference readings

Sustained high moisture is more important than a temporarily elevated reading immediately after rainfall.

---

# Mold and Attic Microbial Growth

Dark discoloration is not enough to identify a specific mold species.

The useful transaction questions are:

* Is visible growth present?
* Is the material currently wet?
* Where is the moisture coming from?
* Has the source been corrected?
* Is the wood structurally intact?
* Is professional remediation appropriate?

Do not spend thousands treating attic growth while leaving the underlying roof leak, air leak, or condensation problem unresolved.

**Moisture control comes first.**

---

# Attic Ventilation: Count Net Free Area, Not Just Vents

A roof can have multiple vents and still be poorly ventilated.

The useful metric is **Net Free Ventilating Area (NFA)**.

Under the IRC framework, the default minimum ventilation ratio is:

`1 square foot NFA / 150 square feet of vented attic`

A **1/300 ratio** can apply where the code's exception requirements are satisfied.

Local adoption must be verified.

---

## Example: 1,800-Square-Foot Vented Attic

At 1/150:

`1,800 ÷ 150 = 12 sq. ft. NFA`

Convert to square inches:

`12 × 144 = 1,728 sq. in. NFA`

At an allowable 1/300 ratio:

`1,800 ÷ 300 = 6 sq. ft.`

`6 × 144 = 864 sq. in. NFA`

Use manufacturer-published NFA—not the visible dimensions of the vent opening.

---

## Check Intake and Exhaust Balance

A ridge vent cannot exhaust effectively if soffit intake is blocked.

Look for:

* Insulation blocking soffits
* Missing baffles
* Crushed baffles
* Painted soffit vents
* Insulation touching roof sheathing at the eaves

Ventilation also does not replace attic air sealing.

Moist indoor air entering through recessed lights, attic hatches, plumbing penetrations, or leaking bath ducts can still create condensation.

# How to Turn Inspection Findings Into Negotiation Leverage

Do not negotiate from the roof's age alone.

Negotiate from **documented scope and cost**.

Assume:

* Roof replacement estimate: **$14,500**
* Flashing reconstruction: included
* Deck replacement: **$95 per sheet**
* Attic staining suggests concealed deck damage
* Exact deck quantity will not be known until tear-off

The transaction discussion should address:

1. The documented $14,500 roof scope
2. Decking unit prices
3. Who carries concealed-condition risk
4. Lender-required repairs
5. Insurance eligibility before closing

That is much stronger than demanding an arbitrary "$20,000 roof credit."

These options are not economically identical.

# Seller Credits: Understand the Mortgage Limits

A $15,000 roof problem does not automatically mean the buyer can receive a **$15,000 unrestricted cash payment** at closing.

Seller concessions are subject to:

* Loan program
* Lender requirements
* Purchase price
* Closing expenses
* Contribution limits

A credit may still be financially useful.

If the seller covers eligible closing expenses, the buyer may preserve cash for the post-closing roof project.

Discuss the structure with the lender before making the credit the centerpiece of the negotiation.

---

# Purchase-Price Reduction Is Not the Same as Repair Money

Reducing the home's price by $15,000 normally does **not** place $15,000 into the buyer's checking account after closing.

For a financed transaction, the monthly payment reduction may be relatively modest.

If the roof needs immediate replacement, verify that the buyer still has:

* Repair liquidity
* Required down payment
* Emergency reserves
* Insurance eligibility

Do not equate a price reduction with a funded roof replacement.

---

# Lender Risk: A Roof Can Delay Closing

Mortgage approval and roof condition are connected.

## Fannie Mae Transactions

Current Fannie Mae guidance treats conditions affecting the property's **safety, soundness, or structural integrity** differently from minor deferred maintenance.

Examples specifically include:

* **Active roof leaks**
* **Worn roof shingles**

Depending on the appraisal and loan structure, completion may need to be verified rather than simply ignored until after closing.

Do not assume the buyer and seller can create their own repair escrow without lender approval.

---

## FHA Roof Condition

FHA guidance requires the roof covering to prevent moisture intrusion and provide reasonable future utility.

If an FHA appraiser determines that the roof has **less than two years of remaining physical life**, the condition must be reported and the appraisal made subject to further inspection by a professional roofer.

That does not mean:

**"Every 20-year-old roof fails FHA."**

Condition matters more than calendar age alone.

# Homeowners Insurance: Get the Quote Before the Inspection Contingency Ends

Mortgage approval does not prove that an insurance company will accept the roof.

Insurance underwriting may consider:

* Roof age
* Material
* Condition
* Number of layers
* Prior claims
* Wind exposure
* Hail exposure
* Remaining useful life
* Existing damage

> **Homebuyer Insurance Rule:** Do not wait until three days before closing to learn that the preferred insurer requires roof replacement.

Get an actual insurance quote during due diligence.

# Ask the Insurance Agent These Questions

Do not ask only:

**"Can you insure the house?"**

Ask:

1. Will you insure the **existing roof without replacement**?
2. Is a post-bind roof inspection required?
3. Is the roof covered at **RCV or ACV**?
4. Is there an age-based depreciation schedule?
5. At what roof age does coverage change?
6. Is there a separate wind/hail deductible?
7. Are cosmetic hail losses excluded?
8. Does the second roof layer affect eligibility?
9. Is proof of installation date required?
10. Could the policy be changed or canceled if an underwriting inspection finds poor roof condition?

Get important answers in writing when possible.

# Avoid the Post-Closing Insurance Surprise

A binder or initial policy does not always mean underwriting is finished.

Insurers can use:

* Exterior inspections
* Aerial imagery
* Property-condition data
* Roof-age databases
* Post-bind inspections

Rules governing cancellation and nonrenewal vary by state.

The practical buyer strategy is to ask explicitly:

**"Is any property or roof inspection still pending after this policy is bound?"**

A buyer does not want to close on Friday and receive a roof-replacement demand shortly afterward.

# 2026 ACV vs. RCV Example for a Homebuyer

Suppose you buy a home with a 15-year-old roof.

Two years later, a covered hailstorm causes a $15,000 replacement loss.

### RCV Policy

Illustratively:

`$15,000 covered replacement - deductible`

Depreciation may initially be withheld but potentially recoverable under policy terms.

### ACV Policy

Illustratively:

`$15,000 - roof depreciation - deductible`

If substantial depreciation applies, the homeowner may have to fund thousands of dollars beyond the deductible.

That is why **roof coverage type can matter almost as much as roof condition**.

---

# A Roof Inspection Is Not the Same as an Insurance Inspection

A professional roof evaluation should assess construction conditions.

The insurer evaluates underwriting and policy eligibility.

Those are different questions.

A roof can be:

* Technically serviceable but difficult to insure
* Insurable but near the end of practical life
* Accepted by a lender but rejected by one insurer
* Rejected by one carrier and acceptable to another

Verify all three separately:

**physical condition + financing + insurance**

---

# Roof Inspection Rating Framework

Use a consistent classification instead of labeling everything "bad."

| Rating                 | Meaning                                                                                           | Buyer action                                       |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| **Pass**               | No material defect observed within inspection limitations                                         | Confirm documentation and insurance                |
| **Minor Attention**    | Localized maintenance/repair item                                                                 | Obtain repair price                                |
| **Major Defect**       | Materially affects remaining life, water shedding, or near-term cost                              | Get specialist estimate before contingency expires |
| **Critical Liability** | Active leak, structural issue, widespread deck damage, near-term replacement or insurance problem | Resolve in writing before closing                  |

# Frequently Asked Questions

## Is a General Home Inspector Enough for the Roof?

Sometimes.

A general inspector can identify broad conditions such as:

* Aging roofing
* Visible leakage
* Attic moisture
* Structural concerns
* Drainage problems
* Flashing defects

A roofing contractor can provide a more detailed assessment of:

* Repairability
* Flashing reconstruction
* Roof measurements
* Replacement scope
* Material availability
* Installed pricing

If the general inspector identifies limited remaining life, active leaks, structural concerns, or systemic deterioration, obtain a written roofing evaluation **before the inspection contingency expires**.

For significant structural deformation or altered trusses, consider a structural engineer.

---

## How Much Does a Roof Inspection Cost in 2026?

A professional roof inspection commonly costs about **$100–$400**, although specialized inspections can cost more.

A structural engineer's roof evaluation can cost more than a basic roofing inspection.

Spending several hundred dollars before closing can be inexpensive compared with discovering a $15,000 roof and $5,000 of decking damage after purchase.

---

## Is a 15-Year-Old Roof Too Old to Buy?

Not automatically.

A documented 15-year-old architectural roof with:

* Good granule retention
* Flat shingles
* Dry decking
* Sound flashing
* Proper ventilation

can present a different risk than a poorly maintained roof of the same age.

But insurance should be checked early.

Some carriers alter roof coverage as the roof ages even when it remains functional.

---

## Does a 10-Year-Old Roof Automatically Become ACV?

No.

There is no nationwide 10-year ACV conversion rule.

Some insurers establish age thresholds at which roof coverage changes from replacement cost to ACV or another depreciation-based structure.

The threshold varies.

Ask the carrier handling the **specific property**.

---

## Is a Second Layer of Shingles a Deal Breaker?

Not necessarily.

It is a financial and inspection issue.

A roof overlay can:

* Conceal deck conditions
* Hide older flashing
* Add dead load
* Increase future tear-off expense

Under the current IRC framework, another recover is not permitted where the roof already has two or more roof-covering applications.

Check local code and permit history.

---

## Can a Seller Give Me a Roof Credit Instead of Replacing It?

Potentially.

The amount and structure of seller credits depend on the mortgage program and lender.

A negotiated $12,000 credit does not automatically mean the buyer can receive $12,000 in cash after closing.

Discuss the proposed concession with the lender before finalizing the inspection response.

---

## Can Roof Repairs Be Escrowed Until After Closing?

Sometimes.

The lender controls whether an escrow holdback is acceptable.

Conditions affecting safety, soundness, structural integrity, or active moisture intrusion can be treated more strictly than minor deferred maintenance.

Obtain written lender approval before assuming a roof repair can wait until after closing.

---

## Does FHA Require a New Roof if It Is Old?

Not simply because of age.

FHA focuses on condition and remaining physical life.

If the appraiser concludes the roof has **less than two years of remaining physical life**, the appraisal is made subject to professional roof inspection under FHA guidance.

A 20-year-old roof does not automatically fail if its condition supports adequate remaining service.

---

## How Can I Tell Whether Roof Deck Staining Is Old or Active?

Look for several clues:

### Potential Active Moisture

* Wet surface
* Elevated moisture readings
* Damp insulation
* Current dripping
* Fresh discoloration

### Historical Leakage

* Dry dark staining
* Rusted fasteners
* Old ceiling patch
* Mineral deposits
* No current moisture

Historical staining still matters.

Ask what failed, whether it was repaired, and whether the wood remains structurally sound.

---

## Does Attic Mold Mean I Should Walk Away From the House?

Not necessarily.

The important issue is the moisture source.

Possible causes include:

* Roof leak
* Blocked ventilation
* Bath fan exhausting into attic
* Interior air leakage
* Condensation

The transaction should address:

1. Moisture source
2. Corrective construction work
3. Structural wood condition
4. Remediation where appropriate

Cleaning visible growth without correcting the moisture source is incomplete.

---

## What If the Seller Does Not Know the Roof Age?

Treat the installation date as **unverified**.

Check:

1. Permit databases
2. Contractor invoices
3. Warranty records
4. Seller disclosures
5. Prior listings
6. Historical imagery
7. Insurance documentation available from the seller

Then make the purchase decision based more heavily on:

* Current condition
* Remaining-life estimate
* Layer count
* Repairability
* Local replacement cost
* Insurance underwriting

Do not invent a roof age to satisfy an insurance application.

---

# Bottom Line: Do Not Buy a Roof Liability Blind

The most dangerous pre-purchase roof is not necessarily the one with the ugliest shingles.

It is the roof whose **cost, remaining life, structural condition, and insurance eligibility are still unknown when the contingency expires**.

Before closing, verify:

1. **Actual roof condition**
2. **Approximate and documented age**
3. **Remaining useful life**
4. **Number of roofing layers**
5. **Flashing condition**
6. **Deck and framing condition**
7. **Attic moisture and ventilation**
8. **Local replacement cost**
9. **Insurance eligibility and RCV/ACV terms**
10. **Lender requirements**
11. **Seller repair or concession structure**
12. **Any local reroofing/code trigger**

A roof problem discovered during inspection is a negotiation issue.

The same problem discovered two weeks after closing is usually **your bill**.
